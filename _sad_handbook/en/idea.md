The script examines the optical system from **light frames** - the frames that are taken in an astrophotography night anyway.
No special test frames are to be needed: no artificial star, no deliberately defocused stars, no focus series and no setup in daylight.
This may cost some ways of analysis; it is a deliberate choice.

### What speaks for it

- **No extra effort.**
  Every frame of a night is a measurement at the same time.
  The diagnosis costs no observing time and needs no extra equipment.
- **The optics at work.**
  The system is measured the way it actually takes pictures: with camera, corrector and filter, at the temperature of that night and in the position in which the telescope points at the sky.
- **Also in hindsight.**
  Older frames can be analyzed later, for example before and after an adjustment or before and after the meridian flip.

### What is lost

- **Errors below the seeing.**
  Seeing and tracking lay a blur over every star.
  Small optical errors that an artificial star or a strongly defocused star disk would show remain hidden in it.
- **The direction of a defocus.**
  A single focused frame shows how blurred a star is, but not whether it is in front of or behind the focus.
  A focus series would settle this; here the only way is to correct in small steps and measure again.
- **Dependence on the star field.**
  The script needs enough well-spread stars up to the corners.
  In sparse fields or with large nebulae, the verdicts become uncertain.

### Tips for good frames

The script gives tips on which frames are well suited for the analysis; they are collected in the chapter [Limits and good practice](#limits).
Most of them apply just as well to good astrophotos: good seeing, clean tracking, short exposures and a rich star field.
The only special point is **what** is analyzed: the single calibrated frame before registration, stacking and processing - not the finished image.
